# Restoration Activities

## Project Areas

### High Park Burn Scar — Larimer County, Colorado

The primary restoration site, located in the area burned by the 2012 High Park Fire. Wildland Ecotech works with dozens of private landowners across 1,000+ acres to restore fire-damaged landscapes.

**The Lazy Lambda** is a 45-acre property purchased by the founder through an agricultural land loan. Steep mountainous terrain with remaining forest patches, a stream with ponds and waterfalls (potable water), and significant fire damage. The property serves as the operational base for restoration work in the area.

**Brown Gulch Watershed** is a 330-acre micro-watershed where restoration work is concentrated. The stream flows through steep terrain accessible via a half-mile hiking trail with 500 feet of elevation change. After storms, the stream can be heard from the trailhead a quarter mile away. The first drone survey of this watershed has been completed, and restoration work is expanding further uphill based on survey data.

**Key activities at this site:**

- Building Beaver Dam Analogs (70+ constructed to date) across multiple properties
- Planting 500+ native trees and shrubs
- Creating hugelkultur terraces from wildfire-reclaimed dead timber
- Developing permaculture food forests on burn scar land
- Building raised beds for annual food crops (food donated to Food Not Bombs Fort Collins)
- Constructing hiking trails using wildfire-reclaimed lumber
- Reducing fuel loads by cutting dead tree limbs (wildfire risk reduction)
- Collecting 1,000+ bags of leaves annually from local neighborhoods for composting
- Installing solar array and batteries on Fish Mountain
- Setting up live streaming camera (in progress)

### GE Moreau Superfund — Upstate New York

An 18-acre property adjacent to the GE Moreau Superfund site. The land is a former sand mine where heavy machinery dug out sand until hitting the water table, creating a bowl-shaped depression now designated as a wetland. Over 50+ years the land has naturally recovered into a series of ponds surrounded by forest, home to fish, turtles, snails, and migratory birds.

The GE Superfund site operates air strippers that pump groundwater and bubble air through it to remove volatile chemicals. The property is bordered by a historic cemetery.

**Current activities:**

- Community partnerships with local ATV trail users for cleanup efforts
- Land stewardship and preservation

**Planned activities:**

- Water and soil sampling to study bioremediation potential (plants, mushrooms, bacteria absorbing and breaking down toxic chemicals)
- Solar panel installation
- Live streaming cameras and microphones for remote species identification
- Solar-powered boat for aquatic ecosystem research

## Programs

### Collecting Leaves

Since 2023, volunteers collect an average of 1,000 bags of leaves every autumn from local neighborhoods. This diverts organic waste from landfills and provides approximately 10 tons of carbon sequestration per year from leaves alone. The collected leaves are used as mulch and compost for protecting young trees and creating water catchment structures.

### Wetland Restoration

The core restoration technique is Low-Tech Process-Based Restoration (PBR), specifically Beaver Dam Analogs (BDAs). These structures are built from locally available materials (logs, mud, rocks) to mimic the work beavers do naturally. BDAs slow water in mountain streams, allowing it to soak into the ground, recharge high-elevation aquifers, and bring life back to old springs. By keeping water at higher elevations longer, BDAs reduce drought and flood risk downstream.

BDAs have proven resilient in storms. During a late spring 2026 storm (2 feet of wet snow, approximately 2.5 inches of rain equivalent, roughly 20 million gallons across the 330-acre Brown Gulch watershed), all ponds filled and overflowed through engineered spillways. Several BDAs were completely overtopped and remained intact. The matrix of sticks, rocks, and mud flexes under pressure rather than failing catastrophically like rigid human-made dams.

### Wildfire Mitigation

Dead tree limbs and downed trees in burn scar aftermath are cut and repurposed for trail building, hugelkultur terraces, and BDA construction. This simultaneously provides building materials, reduces fire risk by lowering fuel loads, and sequesters carbon by burying logs in soil. Water catchment structures also serve as natural firebreaks and firefighter staging areas during active wildfires.

### Permaculture

Since 2020, volunteers have worked to transform burn scars into permaculture food forests. The uphill portion of hugelkultur terraces is planted with native perennial plants (trees, shrubs, fruit), while the downhill slope has raised beds for growing annual vegetable crops. Raised beds are built from wildfire-reclaimed dead trees using a Lincoln Log construction method, with metal T-posts for structural support and compost from the leaf collection program as fill material.

Food grown in the raised beds is donated to Food Not Bombs Fort Collins, where the founder cooks and serves free meals to the unhoused community nearly every week.

### Education

Partnership with Colorado State University's STEM 4 Kids program. Hands-on workshops use scale model beaver dams with USB-powered water pumps to demonstrate how beavers create wetlands and filter sediments. Funded by the United We Om grant, supplies are being produced so students and teachers can build their own models for classroom use.

## Drone Surveys

The first drone survey of the Brown Gulch watershed was completed in May 2026. Drone surveys serve multiple purposes:

- Year-over-year change detection to document restoration progress
- Rigorous before-and-after data for grant applications
- Quantitative metrics required by state and federal wildfire protection grant programs (COSWAP, CSFS FRWRM, CWDG)
- Trail mapping, fuel reduction documentation, and volunteer hour correlation

After collecting the initial survey data, restoration work has begun expanding further up the Brown Gulch watershed.

## Grant Qualification Strategy

Documented metrics from drone surveys, volunteer hour logs, and restoration activity tracking are critical for qualifying for state and federal grants:

- **COSWAP** (Colorado Strategic Wildfire Action Program) — Larimer County is an explicit strategic focus area; 501(c)(3) nonprofits are eligible; crew time awards require no match; cash grants up to $200,000 with 25% match (volunteer hours count at $38.74/hour per CSFS)
- **CSFS FRWRM** (Forest Restoration & Wildfire Risk Mitigation) — ~$7M per cycle; volunteer labor counts as match
- **CWCB** (Watershed Restoration & Flood Mitigation) — $15M available; rolling applications
- **RESTORE Colorado** (NFWF) — Explicitly funds beaver mimicry structures; $100K minimum grants

Note: Volunteer hour matching applies only to hours contributed during a grant's project period, not retroactively. Past volunteer hours demonstrate organizational capacity in applications.
